Topics

About this map

Reading it

  • A line joins the institution a paper was written from to a place it studies. Its width is the number of papers on it. With all authors, each paper is shared among its authors' institutions; with the corresponding author it comes from one. Lines whose ends fall in the same clusters merge, so the map shows fewer, thicker lines at world scale and splits them as you zoom in.
  • Shape says what kind of place is studied: a circle for an area (a country, a province, a basin), a triangle for a natural feature (a river, a lake, a peak), a diamond for a settlement. Dark circles are institutions, merged into clusters that split as you zoom.
  • Colour is the setting the papers state about their study: urban, rural or mixed, from a quoted phrase in the paper. A mark is grey when most of its papers give no clear setting. Switch to "Kind of place" to colour by shape class instead.
  • Size is papers, on one scale for every circle on the map. Each paper counts once, shared among its institutions and among its places of study.
  • Hover a place for the setting its papers state, the topics they carry, and, for a country or province, how its people split between urban centres, towns and rural areas.

How the places were found

  1. Origins. For each paper with a DOI, OpenAlex lists every author's institutions, with coordinates. When an author carries both a parent body and a unit, the one named in the author's own affiliation is kept.
  2. Places in the abstract. Capitalised word runs in each title, abstract and keyword list are matched to a GeoNames gazetteer. Homonyms are ranked by feature type, the authors' countries and the other places in the same abstract. A small language model (Claude Haiku) gives each candidate a role (studied, data extent, comparison, context, affiliation, not a place) and the paper one scope class. It can only choose among the candidates.
  3. Places and setting in the body. The same model reads the opening and the study-area or methods section of each paper with enough text (1,792 papers). It may name a place the gazetteer missed, but only with a quotation of 5 to 25 words that is found in the text and contains the name; the place is then matched to the gazetteer. It also names the setting (urban, rural, mixed, not applicable or unclear), again with a quotation. A claim whose quotation is not found is kept but never mapped.
  4. Urban centres, towns and rural areas. Settlements and institutions take the class of their 1 km cell in the GHS Settlement Model grid for 2020. Countries and provinces get the share of their people in each class from the GHS population grid. These are context only: the setting comes from the paper.

What this does not show

  • One reader's library, chosen by interest: it shows their reading, not a field.
  • Papers without a DOI (about 440) have no origin yet, and OpenAlex affiliations are patchy before about 2000.
  • The body reading covers about 8,500 characters a paper, so a study site named only in a later section can be missed.
  • A third of spatial papers state no clear setting, mostly those about whole countries and basins.
  • Years are binned to five, and places named by only one paper are merged into their province or country.
  • The corresponding author is a publishing convention, so the all-authors view sits beside it.
