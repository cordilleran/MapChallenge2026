A dryland hillslope, four thousand years of storms

A 600 m square of dryland: a mountain front about 80 m high and the apron below it, shaped by soil creep and by the water of short, intense storms. Patchy shrubs shield the soil from wash, soak up water running off the bare ground above them and catch its sediment. Each process is a Landlab component or a short rule written for this page, described on its card below.     Reading the lab

    What the model assumes

    The height of each 5 m cell changes for three reasons, written as one budget:

    ∂z/∂t = D∇²z − E + Dep

    Creep (D∇²z) moves soil downhill in proportion to slope. Its net effect at a cell depends on how the slope bends. ∇²z is the curvature: how far a cell sits below the average of its neighbours (positive, a hollow) or above it (negative, a crest). So a convex crest sheds more than it receives and lowers, and a concave hollow fills.

    Wash lifts soil at a rate E = K Qm S, set by the discharge Q flowing through a cell and its slope S. It drops soil at a rate Dep = vₛ × (sediment carried ÷ water carried), faster where the water carries a lot for its volume.

    The exponent m decides how much bigger flows count. Doubling the discharge multiplies the cutting rate by 2m: ×1.4 at m 0.5, ×1.7 at 0.75 (the baseline), ×2 at m 1. When big flows dominate, they deepen their own paths and capture their neighbours' water, and gullies form.

    Water only runs off when a storm drops more than the ground can soak up, and shrub patches soak up some of what runs onto them. Nothing grows or dies; the shrub pattern is fixed.

    Landforms to look for

    • A creep-rounded crest, the top 15 m. Little water has gathered there, so creep does most of the work and rounds the edge. A few cells lower, wash takes over.
    • Branching gullies on the mountain front, deepest where several rills have joined. On the Change layer they are the darkest warm lines.
    • Deposits at the gully mouths, where the slope eases from 30 % to 2 % and the water can no longer carry its load.
    • Washes across the apron, the few paths that carry the gullies' water to the open edge. The Flow paths layer shows them.
    • Shrub islands: on the front, a dense patch loses about a metre less than bare ground with the same water draining to it, and gullies tend to run around patches. That is small next to a 10 m gully, but you can see it on the Change layer.

    Things to try

    1. Choose Change, set time to 0 and press Play. Watch where cutting starts and how the gullies lengthen and branch.
    2. Compare Wash m 0.5 with Wash m 1. Same storms, same erodibility at a typical discharge; only the weight given to big flows changes.
    3. Try Rain ×2 and read the budget chart against the baseline.
    4. Compare Cover ×0 and Cover ×2 on the Shrubs layer, then on Change.
    5. Lock a cell in a gully (click), then scrub time and watch both profiles. The dashed line is the starting surface.

    How a landscape model runs in Python

    Most landscape evolution models do one thing over and over. They hold the ground surface as a grid of heights, work out how much material each process moves in a short time step, update the grid, and repeat. NumPy does the arithmetic on whole grids at once, so a step over 14,400 cells takes milliseconds.

    Landlab, a Python library from the Community Surface Dynamics Modeling System (Hobley et al., 2017; Barnhart et al., 2020), supplies the grid itself: its nodes, the links between them, and named fields stored on both, such as topographic__elevation. It also supplies components, each a published process law with a run_one_step method that reads and writes those fields. A model is then a short loop that calls the components in turn on one shared grid. The core of this page's loop, slightly simplified:

    # the grid: 120 x 120 nodes, 5 m apart; the bottom edge is open, the other three closed
# 800 steps of 5 years
for step in range(800):
    # every storm in those 5 years, one at a time
    for storm in storms_in(step):
        runoff[:] = max(storm.depth - soak_up, 0) / storm.duration
        # FlowAccumulator: route the water, add up discharge downslope
        flow.run_one_step()
        # shrub patches drink water arriving from upslope
        discharge[:] = runon(runoff, soak_up)
        # ErosionDeposition: lift and drop sediment for the length of the storm
        wash.run_one_step(storm.duration)
    # LinearDiffuser: soil creep over the 5 years
    creep.run_one_step(5.0)

    The full script, day-10/sim.py, is about 270 lines; python sim.py --seed 42 --out runs/d42 runs 2,000 years in about 30 seconds. It also writes the starting surface, the storms and a volume ledger. The ledger counts the sediment that wash carries out through the open edge as it goes, and checks that the surface lost exactly that much plus what creep moved across the edge. The creep share is taken from the change in volume over each creep step, so the ledger is a check on the wash bookkeeping, not an independent check of creep; the benchmark's scarp and closed-bowl checks test creep from outside.

    Four are Landlab components; two are rules written for this page, the runoff rule and the shrub pattern. Each card states the process in words, gives its equation and what the equation means, and lists the parameter values these runs used.

    About this model: method and limits

    How a run is set up

    The starting surface is a 30 % mountain front over a 2 % apron, smoothed where they meet, with seeded roughness at two scales and five shallow hollows, 3 m deep at the top and fading downslope. Real mountain fronts inherit relief like this from earlier storms and from the rock. Without it, water starts on a nearly planar slope and spreads into many parallel rills. In tests the hollows alone changed little: they decide where gullies start, and the wash exponent decides whether they grow. The starting surface is pit-filled once, so water can run off it from the first storm.

    Storms are drawn for the whole run before it starts: about twelve a year, two hours long on average, with an average depth of 14 mm. The rain scenarios multiply every storm's depth, so the storms fall on the same days in every scenario. Wash runs storm by storm; creep runs every five years. Halving every step changes the exported volume by 0.3 %, well inside the 10 % that benchmark check C9 allows.

    Why these gullies, and why the wash exponent is a scenario

    An earlier version of this page used m 0.5, the value common in models of river incision, and ran 2,000 years. It cut many short, parallel rills. Argon's entry in the benchmark (below) drew deeper, branching canyons for three reasons: its starting surface had three hollows cut into it, its wash law raised discharge to the power 1.3, and it ran 4,000 years.

    This version raises m to 0.75, adds the five hollows and runs 4,000 years. Erodibility is set so that the cutting rate is the same, whatever m is, at a typical storm discharge (55,000 m³ a year, the runoff of about a hundred cells). Above that discharge a higher m also cuts more in total, so part of the extra depth at m 1 is more erosion, not only more concentration. In this model, how strongly big flows dominate decides whether the slope grows rills or gullies. The idea goes back to Smith and Bretherton (1972), and Tucker and Bras (1998) relate it to how densely channels cover a slope. The exponent for overland flow is not well constrained, so the baseline value is partly chosen so the gullies are easy to see. That is why both ends of the range are on the page.

    What this model leaves out

    • Water follows one steepest path per cell (D8), so channels run along the grid's eight directions.
    • The shrubs do not grow, spread or die: the pattern is fixed for the whole run. In real drylands, patches and runoff shape each other (Ludwig et al., 2005).
    • Runoff is the storm depth minus a soak-up depth. There is no soil moisture between storms and no intensity peak within one.
    • The settling term is a net effective value, not a grain property (Shobe et al., 2017). Its rise under shrubs is an assumption.
    • Shrubs matter less on the front than at the foot. Wash carried off the upper slope falls only about 5 % from bare ground to double cover (benchmark check C8, on a 2,000-year run), because the gullies, which run past the patches, carry most of it. Export at the open edge falls about 30 % from bare ground to baseline cover, then hardly at all with more.
    • Storm depths come from a gamma distribution with no upper limit. Over 4,000 years the deepest storm is 550 mm, far beyond any real two-hour dryland storm.
    • The top row of cells is a fixed edge and never changes, so a small step stands above the rounded crest.
    • The checks test behaviour, not whether these are the right equations for a particular dryland. The rates are illustrative, not calibrated to a site.

    How this page was tested: the benchmark sprint10 to 12 November

    Why a benchmark

    The code behind this page was written with an AI coding agent, and agents now write a good share of research code. Code that runs is not the same as code that gets the geography right. A simulator can delete sediment, let water climb out of a closed basin, or report a perfect mass balance it never computed, and still draw a handsome map. So before this page was built, the same assignment went to several agents as a one-shot test. The aim was to learn which kinds of mistake they make, not to crown a winner.

    The assignment

    Write a Python script, sim.py, that simulates a dryland hillslope of at least 100 by 100 cells under four coupled processes: soil creep, erosion by overland flow with deposition downslope, random storms, and patchy vegetation that changes how easily the surface erodes. The script must take fixed command-line options (a seed, a starting surface, scales for rain and vegetation, a time-step scale) and save its grids. Then build an interactive map for a student. The equations, parameters and libraries were left to the agent.

    How an entry is scored

    A test harness runs each script in a sealed container (no network, one processor, 2 GB of memory). It runs the entry on its own default surface and on surfaces the harness supplies, then recomputes everything from the saved grids. It never takes the script's word for a number.     What happened

    1. 10 Nov
      A draft benchmark. A chat with Gemini produced a first prompt. It fixed the equations and asked each script to report its own mass balance. That tested whether an agent can follow a recipe, and a script can print a mass balance of 0.0 whatever it does. The rewrite left the physics to the agent and moved all measurement into the harness.
    2. 11 Nov
      Round 1: three agents, every check passed, and the landscapes still differed in kind. Gemini 4 Argon and Sonnet 5.5 in two harnesses all passed. One carved canyons and fans, one barely changed. Checks that pass such different models are too weak. A design review by a second model (Claude Fable 5.2) led to version 2. The harness now routes flow itself and re-runs each entry on its test surfaces. To prove that each check can fail, a reference script and seven deliberately broken copies were added: one deletes sediment, one has no creep, one ignores vegetation. Every broken copy must fail at least one check.
    3. 11 Nov
      The page that taught best. Argon's interactive map, with its cross-sections, field guide and budget chart, was the most satisfying to use. This page starts from it, with the physics rebuilt on Landlab components.
    4. 12 Nov
      Round 2: the check was wrong, not the models. Opus 5.5 in two harnesses, Gemini 3.8 Flash and the first Landlab build were scored. Check C4 failed three of the four. C4 took the cells with the least upslope area, which on the test hillslope is the crest. It required their change to follow curvature, the signature of creep: convex ground loses, concave ground gains. Splitting each run into creep alone and wash alone showed every entry's creep had that signature almost perfectly (rank correlation 0.96 to 0.97). Wash does the opposite, and should. At the very top the ground is most convex, but little water has gathered and the slope is still gentle, so wash cuts least there and more with each step downslope. So C4 passed models whose crest wash was weak and failed those where it was vigorous. It measured a balance between two processes, not an error. C4 became a reported description. The test set gained "correct but different" copies that must pass. Two do (wash ten times stronger, creep five times weaker). A third, with a threshold for wash, still fails C8, the shrub check; a change to C8 that would clear it is written up and waits for review.
    5. 12 Nov
      Round 3: shrubs that catch water. In the Landlab build, shrub patches now soak up runoff from the bare ground above them and trap its sediment. Scored beside Gemini 4 Hydrogen, released that day.
    6. 12 Nov
      This page. The wash exponent rose from 0.5 to 0.75 and the starting surface gained hollows, so gullies form and branch (see "About this model"). The build was scored again with the same checks.

    A check can catch every fault and still fail good models. The broken copies tested whether each check could fail. Nothing tested whether a correct but different model would pass, until C4 failed three of them.

    Domain tooling brings the physics, not the speed. Two Landlab components were too slow for the benchmark's limit: about 11,500 runoff storms in a 2,000-year run, within a minute. The priority-flood router took 80 ms a storm and was replaced by routing on a surface filled once at the start. The lossy flow accumulator took 67 ms, because it calls a Python function at every node; its rule was rewritten in vectorised form (0.4 ms) and tested equal to the component.
